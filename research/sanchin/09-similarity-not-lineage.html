Similarity Is Not Lineage Proof

This is the archive's governing rule. Similar movement, terminology, breathing, stepping, or complementary-force ideas can justify comparison. Historical descent requires a stronger chain of evidence.

What similarity can showWhat lineage requiresEvolution and convergenceArchive standard

What similarity can show

Repeated similarities can make coincidence less likely and can identify promising research families. Geography, chronology, terminology, foundational-form status, directional patterns, and structural themes can collectively establish a serious comparative case.

Archive standard: resemblance creates a research obligation, not a permission to invent a genealogy.

What historical lineage requires

A direct lineage claim becomes stronger when it can identify people, dates, locations, texts, photographs, school records, correspondence, inscriptions, or mutually supporting testimony. The more independent links survive, the more confidently a transmission can be described.

Where those links are absent, the archive uses terms such as comparative, traditional identification, practitioner testimony, or open inquiry instead of presenting uncertainty as fact.

Evolution, loss, and convergence

Related traditions can diverge over generations. Unrelated traditions can also converge on similar solutions. A third possibility is partial preservation: an older training principle survives while the visible form changes. The archive keeps all three possibilities available until chronology and documentation narrow them.

The standard carried into every dossier

History first. Comparison second. Conclusion only where evidence permits. This does not weaken the investigation; it makes every genuine connection more valuable because readers can see exactly why the archive considers it credible.
