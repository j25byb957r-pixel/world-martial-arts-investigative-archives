Sanchin, Seisan, Sanseiryu

The three-form core associated with Kanbun Uechi gives the archive a stable internal framework for studying transmission. Later additions matter, but they should not obscure the distinction between the earlier three-kata core and the curriculum that developed afterward.

The three-form core

Historical Uechi material consistently identifies Sanchin, Seisan, and Sanseiryu as the central kata associated with Kanbun Uechi's Wakayama teaching. This makes the trio a better starting point for historical comparison than the full modern curriculum, which includes forms developed later.

Archive classification: strongly supported within Uechi historical sources. The distinction between Kanbun's three-form core and later system development is essential when tracing possible Fujian relationships.

Why Sanchin receives special attention

Sanchin is repeatedly treated as foundational. That does not mean it contains every later technical expression, but it does mean that changes in its stepping, breathing, structural organization, or teaching sequence deserve careful attention because they may reveal how the system was transmitted and reorganized.

Later development does not invalidate the earlier core

Postwar Uechi-Ryū expanded its teaching curriculum with additional kata and training structures. Those additions are part of the living history of the art. For research purposes, however, the archive marks them separately so a later pedagogical development is not accidentally projected backward into Kanbun's Chinese period.

Why the three-form distinction helps our investigation

When comparing Fujian material, the strongest question is not whether a modern Southern Chinese school resembles all modern Uechi kata. It is whether historically datable Fujian practices provide credible parallels to the earlier three-form core, and whether those parallels can be connected through people, places, or records rather than resemblance alone.
