The Sanchin Question: Fujian → Okinawa

A source-conscious investigation of Sanchin, San Zhan / Sam Chien, Kanbun Uechi’s transmitted kata, and the unresolved relationship between Fujian boxing and Okinawan Uechi-Ryū. The archive distinguishes documentary history, traditional identification, comparative evidence, practitioner testimony, and open inquiry.

01 • Historical baselineDocumented tradition

Kanbun Uechi and Fujian

Uechi historical accounts place Kanbun Uechi in Fujian beginning in the late 1890s and associate his Chinese training with the material later transmitted in Japan and Okinawa. The broad Fujian connection is central to Uechi tradition, while details of the exact school, teacher record, and curriculum remain historically difficult to prove.

02 • TerminologyComparative

San Zhan / Sam Chien / Sanchin

Related Southern Chinese and Okinawan names are commonly rendered San Zhan, Sam Chien, and Sanchin and are associated with the idea of “three battles” or “three conflicts.” Linguistic resemblance is meaningful evidence for comparison, but it does not by itself establish a single teacher-to-student transmission.

03 • Core curriculumStrongly supported

Sanchin, Seisan, Sanseiryu

Historical Uechi material consistently identifies Sanchin, Seisan, and Sanseiryu as the three core kata of Kanbun Uechi’s Wakayama-era teaching. Later Uechi-Ryū added bridging kata, making the distinction between Kanbun’s three-form core and later system development important to the archive.

04 • Early trainingHistorical account

Forward and backward study

Accounts of the Wakayama teaching period describe beginners spending an extended introductory period on Sanchin fundamentals, including movement forward and backward. This makes directional continuity a legitimate historical research question. It does not, by itself, prove that every later “three forward / three back” interpretation came unchanged from Fujian.

05 • Fujian relationshipProbable region; exact line unresolved

A family resemblance, not a solved genealogy

Graham Noble’s historical investigation argues that Okinawan Sanchin almost certainly has Fujian roots while emphasizing that present-day Fujian forms do not provide a clean match for Uechi Sanchin. This is a useful model for the archive: regional relationship can be strongly plausible even when exact ancestry remains unresolved.

06 • White CraneComparative evidence

Southern White Crane is a comparison, not a verdict

White Crane traditions provide important San Zhan material and broader Fujian context. Similar terminology, compact structure, coordinated breathing, and Southern Chinese training conventions justify careful comparison. The archive will not convert those parallels into a claim that White Crane is definitively the direct parent of Uechi-Ryū without a traceable historical chain.

07 • PangainoonUnresolved

The name remains a historical problem

“Pangainoon” is traditionally explained as a hard/soft or half-hard/half-soft designation. Modern historical research has raised substantial questions about when the name and its written Chinese identification entered the record and whether a Chinese school using that exact name can be independently established. The archive therefore preserves the traditional explanation while labeling its provenance unresolved.

08 • TransmissionDocumented development

Okinawa did not simply freeze the Wakayama curriculum

Later Uechi-Ryū developed additional kata and organized teaching structures around the original core. Historical accounts also report differences between Kanbun-era and later practice. The research question is therefore not merely “Where did Sanchin originate?” but also “What was retained, reorganized, emphasized, or changed during transmission?”

09 • MethodArchive standard

Similarity is not lineage proof

A shared name, posture, directional idea, breathing emphasis, or complementary-force principle can justify comparison. Historical descent requires more: identifiable people, dates, locations, texts, photographs, records, or mutually supporting testimony. Where that chain is absent, the archive says so.

Heaven & Earth research matrix

Comparison without retroactive attribution

The archive will next test the user’s Heaven-and-Earth framework against historically observable complementary relationships: upper and lower organization, rising and sinking, opening and closing, expansion and contraction, hard and soft, advance and retreat, and maintenance of centered structure while direction changes. These may prove useful as comparative categories. They are not evidence that Kanbun Uechi, a Fujian teacher, or later American pioneers used one named doctrine called “Heaven and Earth” unless a source demonstrates that terminology.

Questions carried forward

What the evidence still has to answer

Which late nineteenth-century Fujian traditions provide the closest historically datable parallels to Uechi Sanchin? How old are the surviving San Zhan versions that are now used for comparison? Can any source bridge Kanbun’s three kata to a specific Fujian curriculum rather than a broad regional family? Which features belong to Kanbun’s transmission, which to later Okinawan development, and which are widespread Southern Chinese conventions? These remain questions, not predetermined conclusions.
