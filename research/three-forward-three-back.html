Three Forward / Three Back

An evidence-led inquiry into bidirectional Sanchin and San Zhan training. The question is evolutionary: which structural features persist across Fujian, Wakayama, Okinawa, and later Uechi-Ryū, which changed, and where does the documentary trail become uncertain?

Yongchun San Zhan explicitly preserves three forward and three back

The published movement record contains 進三步同 and 退三步同前, while identified modern Yongchun audiovisual material independently presents San Zhan as a three-advance/three-retreat practice. Together these establish a living transmission pattern; neither source by itself dates the choreography to the Qing period.

Early Uechi training emphasized forward and backward Sanchin

The Footprints of Kanbun Uechi records that new students in Wakayama spent their first three months practicing movement forward and backward in Sanchin. This makes bidirectional study historically relevant without proving that its exact count matched the modern Yongchun sequence.

Secondary bibliographic descriptions report Qing-era White Crane texts, including works dated around 1795–1796 and a late-Qing manual reported to recommend Three Battles as the first practice set. Chronological test

What the timeline currently permits

c. 1795–1796: secondary descriptions report White Crane manuals from this period. They do not yet prove that those particular dated manuals contain the exact 3+3 choreography.

Late Qing: a reported White Crane teaching text places Three Battles first in instruction. Confirmation from the underlying document would securely predate Kanbun Uechi's Fujian period.

1897–1910: Uechi tradition places Kanbun's Fujian study in this period. Pre-1897 San Zhan evidence is therefore important, but a transmission bridge is still required before claiming ancestry.

Wakayama: retrospective Uechi history preserves forward-and-backward Sanchin as basic early training.

Modern Yongchun: written and audiovisual presentations preserve three advancing and three retreating steps. This demonstrates survival, not original date.

Evidence discipline

Chronology is not genealogy

An earlier regional practice and a later similar practice can be related in several ways: shared convention, parallel development, ancestor, or independently preserved relative. The archive does not choose among those possibilities without a defensible transmission chain.

Evolutionary map

Continuity without assuming identity

Late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Fujian: White Crane manuscript traditions are reported from the 1790s onward, with a late-Qing source reportedly prioritizing San Zhan.

Kanbun's Fujian period: the task is to identify regional teachers, lineages, and San Zhan variants demonstrably accessible during 1897–1910.

Wakayama: early Uechi accounts preserve bidirectional Sanchin but do not yet give enough detail to equate its choreography with Yongchun San Zhan.

Okinawan systematization: Sanchin remains foundational while the broader curriculum develops.

Modern comparison: count, direction, posture, rhythm, and other visible features can be compared. Similarity and difference are both recorded.

Structural interpretation

Advance and retreat as one organized relationship

The project's working interpretation considers whether direction can reverse while central organization remains continuous. This can be compared with paired concepts such as opening/closing, rising/sinking, expansion/contraction, and hard/soft. The language remains interpretive unless a historical source explicitly uses the same framework.

Next evidentiary bridge

The manuscripts behind the manuscript claims

The highest-value next step remains primary or near-primary evidence: title pages, manuscript photographs, catalog records, scholarly editions, dated colophons, provenance notes, and transcriptions. Of special value is any securely dated pre-1897 text containing 三戰, describing its instructional priority, or recording advance/retreat choreography.
