Movement-history dossier

Three Forward / Three Back

An evidence-led inquiry into bidirectional Sanchin and San Zhan training. The central question is evolutionary: which structural features persist across Fujian, Wakayama, Okinawa, and later Uechi-Ryū, which were modified, and where does the documentary trail become uncertain?

Fujian transmission sourceCorroborated pattern

A Yongchun White Crane San Zhan sequence explicitly records three forward and three back

FujianBaihe's published San Zhan movement list contains the Chinese instructions 進三步同 and 退三步同前, recording a three-step advance followed by a three-step retreat sequence. This strengthens the numerical pattern from a general comparison into a documented feature of at least one living Yongchun White Crane transmission.

The page itself does not establish when the underlying sequence was first written, so the archive does not use it to claim that the exact 3+3 count can already be dated to Kanbun Uechi's years in Fujian.

Early Uechi training also emphasized forward and backward Sanchin

The Footprints of Kanbun Uechi records that new students in Kanbun Uechi's Wakayama dojo spent their first three months practicing only movement forward and backward in Sanchin. This is strong evidence that bidirectional movement was not merely a later reconstruction.

Evidence upgrade

What has changed in the archive's classification

The archive no longer treats three forward / three back as merely a personal observation or an isolated modern anecdote. We now have: a published Yongchun White Crane San Zhan sequence that explicitly records three advancing and three retreating steps; independent present-day White Crane descriptions of San Zhan as a three-step advancing/retreating practice; and a retrospective Wakayama account documenting forward/backward Sanchin as an early Uechi training method.

What remains unproven is the transmission bridge. None of these sources yet demonstrates that Kanbun Uechi learned this exact 3+3 choreography from a particular White Crane teacher or that the surviving Yongchun wording predates his Fujian training.

Evolutionary map

Continuity without assuming identity

Stage 1 — Fujian environment: San Zhan appears as a foundational form in Yongchun White Crane, with surviving transmission material that includes a three-forward/three-back sequence.

Stage 2 — Kanbun / Wakayama: early Uechi historical accounts preserve forward-and-backward Sanchin practice, but the surviving source does not give enough detail to equate its choreography with the White Crane sequence.

Stage 3 — Okinawan systematization: Sanchin remains foundational while the broader Uechi curriculum is organized and expanded. Directional practice should therefore be studied both as inherited material and as something potentially reshaped by later pedagogy.

Stage 4 — modern comparison: similarities in count, direction, central structure, breath, and hard/soft organization become evidence for comparison—not automatic proof of a single lineage.

Structural interpretation

Advance and retreat as one organized relationship

The project's working interpretation is that direction can reverse while central organization remains continuous. This offers a useful way to compare advance/retreat with opening/closing, rising/sinking, expansion/contraction, and hard/soft. Within the broader Heaven-and-Earth inquiry, these paired relationships can be studied as complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

This language remains explicitly interpretive unless a historical source uses the same conceptual framework.

Next evidentiary bridge

The question is now one of dating

The highest-value next step is not finding more modern examples. It is identifying the earliest datable Fujian records that contain San Zhan choreography, terminology, or the three-step advance/retreat pattern. If a source can be securely placed before or during Kanbun Uechi's Fujian period, the historical comparison becomes substantially stronger. If no such source survives, that absence must remain visible in the archive.
