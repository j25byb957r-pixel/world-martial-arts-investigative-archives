Movement-history dossier

Three Forward / Three Back

An evidence-led inquiry into forward-and-backward Sanchin training, directional symmetry, and related San Zhan traditions. The archive records what historical and practitioner sources actually say, then keeps structural interpretation separate from lineage claims.

Wakayama recordDocumented in later history

Forward and backward Sanchin was a basic early exercise

A historical account preserved in The Footprints of Kanbun Uechi states that new students in Kanbun Uechi's Wakayama dojo spent their first three months practicing only movement forward and backward in Sanchin. This is strong evidence that bidirectional Sanchin movement belonged to the early teaching culture associated with Kanbun.

San Zhan also preserves forward/backward movement

Modern White Crane practitioner material describes San Zhan as teaching posture while moving both forward and backward. This establishes a meaningful Fujian comparison, but it does not by itself prove that Uechi Sanchin inherited the pattern directly from one specific White Crane lineage.

Does “three forward / three back” belong to an older shared pattern?

Some practitioner discussions describe versions of Sam Chien / San Zhan with three steps forward and three back. This is worth preserving as a research lead, but the present archive does not treat the exact 3+3 count as universally documented across all Fujian or Okinawan lineages.

What the evidence supports

Direction mattered before later reconstruction

The clearest current point is not a mystical numerical claim but a historical training fact: early Wakayama accounts describe Sanchin as something practiced in both directions. Comparable White Crane material also treats forward and backward travel as part of San Zhan training. Together, these sources justify investigating directional balance as an older Southern Chinese/Okinawan training concern.

They do not yet establish a single original choreography, a universal three-step count, or one direct line of descent from a named White Crane school to Uechi-Ryū.

Comparative interpretation

Frontal organization and complementary direction

The archive will track a recurring structural observation: a form may move toward or away from a reference point while preserving an organized forward-facing body relationship. This can be compared conceptually with paired ideas such as advance/retreat, opening/closing, rising/sinking, and hard/soft.

Within the project's broader Heaven-and-Earth inquiry, forward and backward movement can therefore be studied as complementary directions rather than as opposites that require abandoning central organization. This remains an interpretive framework unless historical sources explicitly describe the same concept.

Research questions

Questions now on the record

Which surviving Fujian San Zhan lineages explicitly preserve three-forward/three-back counts? How early can those counts be documented? Did Wakayama Sanchin preserve a longer bidirectional training method than later standardized kata performance? Are similar counts independent teaching conventions, regional inheritance, or evidence of closer historical relationship?
