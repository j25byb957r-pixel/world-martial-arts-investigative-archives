Movement-history dossier

Three Forward / Three Back

An evidence-led inquiry into bidirectional Sanchin and San Zhan training. The central question is evolutionary: which structural features persist across Fujian, Wakayama, Okinawa, and later Uechi-Ryū, which were modified, and where does the documentary trail become uncertain?

Fujian transmission sourceCorroborated pattern

A Yongchun White Crane San Zhan sequence explicitly records three forward and three back

FujianBaihe's published San Zhan movement list contains the Chinese instructions 進三步同 and 退三步同前, recording a three-step advance followed by a three-step retreat sequence. This strengthens the numerical pattern from a general comparison into a documented feature of at least one living Yongchun White Crane transmission.

The page itself does not establish when the underlying sequence was first written, so the archive does not use it to claim that the exact 3+3 count can already be dated to Kanbun Uechi's years in Fujian.

Early Uechi training also emphasized forward and backward Sanchin

The Footprints of Kanbun Uechi records that new students in Kanbun Uechi's Wakayama dojo spent their first three months practicing only movement forward and backward in Sanchin. This is strong evidence that bidirectional movement was not merely a later reconstruction.

Late-Qing White Crane manuscript traditions may move San Zhan much earlier

A specialist bookseller's description of a collected White Crane manuscript set reports several Qing-era texts, including works dated around 1795–1796 and an unattributed late-Qing manual titled around the founder Fang Qiniang. That description specifically says the late-Qing manual recommends Three Battles as the first practice set.

This is potentially important chronological evidence because it places San Zhan terminology and foundational status before Kanbun Uechi's Fujian period. However, the archive classifies it as a secondary bibliographic report until the underlying manuscript images, catalog record, edition history, or scholarly transcription can be independently examined.

Chronological test

What the timeline currently allows us to say

c. 1795–1796: secondary bibliographic descriptions report surviving White Crane manuals from this period. These establish that White Crane theory was being written down by the late eighteenth century, but the descriptions currently available do not prove that those particular dated manuals contain the exact 3+3 choreography.

Late Qing dynasty: the same bibliographic source describes an unattributed White Crane manual that recommends Three Battles as the first practice set. If the dating and manuscript description are confirmed from the underlying source, this would place San Zhan's foundational status securely before Kanbun Uechi's arrival in Fujian.

1897–1910: Uechi-Ryū tradition places Kanbun Uechi's Fujian study in this period. The archive therefore treats pre-1897 San Zhan evidence as especially significant, while still requiring a transmission link before claiming direct ancestry.

Wakayama period: retrospective Uechi histories preserve forward-and-backward Sanchin as basic early training. This provides continuity on the Uechi side, but the exact numerical choreography remains incompletely documented.

Modern Yongchun transmission: published San Zhan material explicitly preserves three advancing and three retreating steps. This confirms survival of the pattern, but not by itself its date of origin.

Evidence discipline

Chronology is not genealogy

The emerging chronology is meaningful: San Zhan is present in White Crane manuscript tradition before the twentieth century, Kanbun Uechi studied in Fujian around the turn of that century, early Uechi training preserved bidirectional Sanchin, and modern Yongchun San Zhan preserves an explicit 3+3 sequence.

But chronology alone does not prove teacher-to-student transmission. A historically earlier practice can be a regional parallel, a shared convention, an ancestor, or an independently preserved cousin. The archive will not choose among those possibilities until the evidence permits it.

Evolutionary map

Continuity without assuming identity

Stage 1 — late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Fujian: White Crane manuscript traditions are reported in sources dated from the 1790s onward; a late-Qing manual reportedly places Three Battles first in instruction.

Stage 2 — Kanbun's Fujian period: Uechi tradition places his study in Fujian from 1897 to 1910. The research task is to identify which regional lineages, teachers, and San Zhan variants were actually accessible to him.

Stage 3 — Kanbun / Wakayama: early Uechi historical accounts preserve forward-and-backward Sanchin practice, but the surviving source does not give enough detail to equate its choreography with the White Crane sequence.

Stage 4 — Okinawan systematization: Sanchin remains foundational while the broader Uechi curriculum is organized and expanded. Directional practice should therefore be studied both as inherited material and as something potentially reshaped by later pedagogy.

Stage 5 — modern comparison: similarities in count, direction, central structure, breath, and hard/soft organization become evidence for comparison—not automatic proof of a single lineage.

Structural interpretation

Advance and retreat as one organized relationship

The project's working interpretation is that direction can reverse while central organization remains continuous. This offers a useful way to compare advance/retreat with opening/closing, rising/sinking, expansion/contraction, and hard/soft. Within the broader Heaven-and-Earth inquiry, these paired relationships can be studied as complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

This language remains explicitly interpretive unless a historical source uses the same conceptual framework.

Next evidentiary bridge

We now need the manuscripts behind the manuscript claims

The highest-value next step is to move from secondary descriptions to the underlying evidence: title pages, manuscript photographs, library or museum catalog records, scholarly editions, dated colophons, provenance notes, and transcriptions. Of special value would be any securely dated pre-1897 text that contains 三戰, describes its instructional priority, or records advance/retreat choreography.

If such a source contains an explicit three-forward/three-back sequence, the chronology would become much stronger. If the exact count appears only in later copies, the archive must preserve that distinction.
