Topic key • research method

History

This key explains what the archive means when it labels a subject historical. It separates chronology, lineage tradition, documentary evidence, later testimony, comparison, and interpretation so readers can see how a conclusion was reached.

Chronology

Chronology asks what can be placed in time: a person's presence in a region, a dojo's operation, a dated text, a published photograph, a curriculum described by a witness, or a later historical account. Earlier evidence is important, but an earlier date alone does not prove direct descent.

Transmission

Transmission requires more than resemblance. The strongest cases connect identifiable people, places, dates, documents, organizations, or mutually supporting testimony. Where links are missing, the archive preserves the gap instead of turning possibility into certainty.

Development

A tradition can change while remaining historically related. The archive therefore tracks continuity, modification, standardization, reinterpretation, regional variation, and possible loss instead of assuming that a modern presentation perfectly reproduces an older one.

Comparative study

Comparison is used to identify questions worth investigating. Similar names, structural ideas, directional patterns, or training emphases can justify closer study. They remain comparative evidence until a historical chain supports a genealogical claim.

Why this key appears throughout the archive

Readers may encounter the word history beneath Kanbun Uechi, Fujian, White Crane, Wakayama, Michigan transmission, or a form such as Sanchin. Clicking this key provides the common evidentiary rules used across those subjects, while the surrounding dossier provides the specific facts and sources.

Working principle: history first, comparison second, conclusion only where evidence permits.
