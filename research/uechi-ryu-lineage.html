Lineage dossier

Uechi-Ryū: Kanbun Uechi, Fujian, and the transmission record

This dossier separates widely repeated lineage history from points that remain uncertain or contested. It is intended as an evolving research record, not a final statement of origin.

1897–1910Documented tradition

Kanbun Uechi in Fujian

Modern Uechi-Ryū organizations consistently preserve the account that Kanbun Uechi left Okinawa in 1897, trained in Fujian for roughly thirteen years, and later taught the art he had learned after returning to Japan.

Ryuyu Tomoyose and renewed teaching

Ryuyu Tomoyose is consistently identified in Uechi-Ryū sources as Kanbun Uechi’s first and senior student in Japan and as the person who persuaded him to resume teaching in Wakayama.

Kanei Uechi and Ryuko Tomoyose

After the war, Kanei Uechi became the central transmitter of the system in Okinawa. Sources describe Ryuko Tomoyose, son of Ryuyu, as an important early student, organizer, interpreter, and later teacher to American servicemen.

Original kata

Sanchin, Seisan, Sanseiryu

Multiple Uechi-Ryū sources identify Sanchin, Seisan, and Sanseiryu as the three kata associated with Kanbun Uechi’s original curriculum. Later intermediate kata were developed within the postwar Uechi-Ryū system. This distinction is useful when comparing Chinese and Okinawan material because it helps isolate the oldest retained core from later pedagogical additions.

Pangainoon problem

A name with unresolved provenance

The term commonly written as “Pangainoon” is central to Uechi-Ryū tradition, but its historical status requires caution. Contemporary Uechi-Ryū sources use it for the art Kanbun studied in China. Other research notes that the precise original Chinese expression, pronunciation, and whether it was actually the formal name of a Fujian style remain uncertain. The modern interpretation “half-hard, half-soft” is influential, but the archive classifies the exact historical provenance of the term as unresolved rather than settled.

Teacher identification

Shū Shiwa / Zhou Zihe

Uechi-Ryū tradition widely identifies Kanbun Uechi’s Chinese teacher as Shū Shiwa, usually equated with Zhou Zihe. The surviving documentation is incomplete, and later investigations in Fujian have produced competing reconstructions of the precise teacher relationship and style classification. Accordingly, this archive treats Zhou Zihe as the traditional identification while keeping the exact historical reconstruction open.

Archive status

What is firm, what remains open

Higher confidence: Kanbun Uechi’s role as founder-name figure; the Fujian-training tradition; Wakayama teaching; the Ryuyu Tomoyose connection; Kanei Uechi’s postwar leadership; Ryuko Tomoyose’s major role in Okinawa and later transmission.

Open inquiry: the exact Chinese system name; the linguistic history of “Pangainoon”; the precise documentary identity and role of Shū Shiwa/Zhou Zihe; and the degree to which present-day Fujian systems preserve the same technical curriculum Kanbun encountered.
